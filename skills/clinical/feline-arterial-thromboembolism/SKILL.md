# Feline Arterial Thromboembolism (FATE)

## Overview

Feline arterial thromboembolism (FATE), commonly associated with cardiogenic disease, is a life-threatening emergency caused by acute arterial obstruction by thrombotic material. The distal aorta and iliac arteries are frequent sites, resulting in ischemia of one or both pelvic limbs.

The condition is commonly associated with cardiomyopathy and left atrial disease. However, the absence of a previously diagnosed cardiac disorder or an audible murmur does not exclude FATE.

This skill supports veterinary professionals in recognizing the syndrome, prioritizing stabilization, selecting diagnostic tests, evaluating treatment options, and monitoring complications.

It is a clinical decision-support resource, not an autonomous diagnostic or prescribing system.

## When to Use

Activate this skill when a cat presents with one or more of the following:

- Sudden onset of pelvic-limb paresis or paralysis.
- Acute, severe limb pain or vocalization.
- Reduced or absent femoral pulses.
- Cold distal limbs or paws.
- Pale, cyanotic, or otherwise abnormal paw pads.
- Acute limb weakness with suspected peripheral ischemia.
- Suspected arterial thromboembolism affecting another vascular territory.

A classic presentation is the combination of acute limb dysfunction, pain, pulselessness, and distal hypothermia. Not all findings are necessarily present in every patient.

## Key Clinical Considerations

### Pathophysiology

FATE is frequently associated with cardiomyopathy, left atrial enlargement, impaired atrial function, and intracardiac thrombus formation.

Thrombotic material may embolize into the systemic arterial circulation, producing acute obstruction and distal tissue ischemia.

Consequences may include:

- Ischemic neuromyopathy.
- Skeletal muscle injury and rhabdomyolysis.
- Increased serum creatine kinase (CK).
- Metabolic disturbances associated with ischemic tissue injury.
- Reperfusion injury following restoration of blood flow.

### Clinical Examination

Assess and document:

1. Onset and progression of clinical signs.
2. Pain severity and response to gentle handling.
3. Motor function and voluntary movement.
4. Femoral pulse quality bilaterally.
5. Distal limb temperature and paw-pad color.
6. Respiratory rate and effort.
7. Heart rate, rhythm, and cardiac auscultation.
8. Rectal temperature and overall perfusion status.

Compare both pelvic limbs whenever possible.

A cardiac murmur may be absent despite clinically significant cardiomyopathy.

## Workflow

### 1. Emergency Triage and Stabilization

Prioritize immediate assessment of:

- Airway and respiratory status.
- Respiratory effort and evidence of pulmonary edema or pleural effusion.
- Cardiovascular stability and perfusion.
- Severe pain.
- Core body temperature.
- Neurologic and limb perfusion abnormalities.

Provide oxygen when clinically indicated.

Initiate appropriate analgesia promptly. Opioid-based analgesia is generally preferred during the acute phase because severe ischemic pain is common.

Avoid unnecessary handling and stressful procedures.

Do not delay stabilization while pursuing advanced imaging.

### 2. Diagnostic Evaluation

FATE is initially a clinical suspicion supported by physical examination and vascular assessment.

Recommended investigations should be selected according to patient stability and available resources.

#### Vascular assessment

- Doppler assessment of arterial blood flow in affected limbs.
- Vascular ultrasonography when available.
- Comparison of perfusion between affected and unaffected limbs.

#### Cardiac assessment

- Focused cardiac ultrasound when appropriate.
- Complete echocardiography after initial stabilization.
- Evaluation of left atrial size and function.
- Assessment for spontaneous echocardiographic contrast or intracardiac thrombus when technically possible.

Thoracic point-of-care ultrasound or thoracic imaging may help identify congestive heart failure, pleural effusion, or pulmonary edema.

#### Laboratory evaluation

Consider:

- CBC.
- Serum biochemistry.
- Electrolytes, particularly potassium.
- Creatine kinase (CK).
- Blood glucose.
- Blood gas analysis when indicated.
- Lactate, interpreted in clinical context.
- Renal parameters and urinalysis when appropriate.

Laboratory results support assessment of systemic consequences and complications but do not independently confirm or exclude FATE.

### 3. Differential Diagnosis

Consider alternative causes of acute pelvic-limb paresis or paralysis:

- Spinal cord injury or intervertebral disc disease.
- Pelvic or limb trauma.
- Fracture or joint luxation.
- Peripheral nerve injury.
- Neuromuscular disease.
- Severe electrolyte abnormalities, including hypokalemia.
- Other causes of acute arterial or vascular compromise.

The combination of absent or reduced arterial pulses and cold distal limbs strongly increases suspicion of arterial ischemia.

### 4. Treatment Planning

Treatment decisions must account for the severity of ischemia, duration of clinical signs, pain, concurrent cardiac disease, respiratory status, and the owner's goals of care.

#### Analgesia

- Provide prompt and adequate analgesia.
- Opioids are commonly used in the acute phase.
- Reassess pain frequently.
- Adjust treatment according to response and adverse effects.

#### Cardiovascular management

Evaluate for concurrent congestive heart failure before administering fluids.

Avoid routine or indiscriminate fluid loading in cats with suspected cardiomyopathy or congestive heart failure.

Fluid therapy, if indicated, must be individualized according to perfusion, hydration, cardiac status, and ongoing losses.

#### Antithrombotic treatment

Antithrombotic therapy is an important component of management and secondary prevention.

Treatment selection should be based on current veterinary evidence, patient-specific risks, and clinician judgment.

Consideration may include:

- Antiplatelet therapy.
- Anticoagulant therapy.
- Combination approaches in selected cases.

Clopidogrel and anticoagulants such as low-molecular-weight heparins or direct oral anticoagulants may be considered according to the clinical context and current evidence.

Do not assume that one regimen is appropriate for every patient.

Thrombolytic therapy is not a routine default intervention. Its potential benefits, risks, timing, and limited evidence base require specialist-level assessment.

Do not generate medication doses unless the required patient information, formulation, route, and an appropriate veterinary reference are available.

### 5. Monitoring and Complications

Monitor closely for:

- Recurrent or progressive ischemia.
- Severe or persistent pain.
- Reperfusion injury.
- Hyperkalemia.
- Metabolic acidosis.
- Rhabdomyolysis.
- Acute kidney injury.
- Cardiac arrhythmias.
- Tissue necrosis or limb viability deterioration.
- Congestive heart failure.

Serial monitoring may include:

- ECG and heart rate.
- Respiratory rate and effort.
- Electrolytes, especially potassium.
- Renal parameters.
- CK trends.
- Acid-base status when indicated.
- Limb temperature, pulse quality, and motor function.
- Urine output in hospitalized patients when clinically appropriate.

Restoration of blood flow does not eliminate the risk of systemic complications.

### 6. Prognosis and Client Communication

Prognosis is variable and depends on:

- Number of affected limbs.
- Severity and duration of ischemia.
- Presence of motor function.
- Response to analgesia and supportive treatment.
- Concurrent cardiomyopathy and congestive heart failure.
- Development of systemic complications.
- Response to initial treatment.

Discuss the possibility of mortality, persistent neurologic deficits, recurrent thromboembolism, and the need for long-term cardiac and antithrombotic management.

Communicate uncertainty honestly. Do not promise recovery based on a single clinical finding.

## Output Format

When assisting with a suspected FATE case, structure the response as follows:

1. **Clinical suspicion:** Findings supporting or opposing FATE.
2. **Immediate priorities:** Stabilization, analgesia, respiratory and cardiovascular assessment.
3. **Diagnostic plan:** Essential tests and their clinical purpose.
4. **Differential diagnoses:** Important alternative explanations.
5. **Treatment considerations:** Individualized analgesia, cardiovascular management, and antithrombotic options.
6. **Monitoring:** Major complications and parameters requiring reassessment.
7. **Prognosis:** Relevant positive and negative prognostic considerations.
8. **Missing information:** Patient data needed before making more specific recommendations.

Clearly distinguish confirmed findings, clinical suspicion, and recommendations requiring further investigation.

## Limitations

- This skill does not replace examination or clinical judgment by a licensed veterinarian.
- A classic clinical presentation does not eliminate the need to consider differential diagnoses.
- Absence of a murmur or known cardiac history does not exclude FATE.
- No universal treatment protocol is appropriate for every patient.
- Evidence for some interventions, particularly thrombolysis, remains limited.
- Drug selection and dosing require patient-specific assessment and verification against current veterinary references.
- Imaging and laboratory testing must be interpreted in the context of the patient's clinical status.
- Local drug regulations, availability, and referral capabilities must be considered.
